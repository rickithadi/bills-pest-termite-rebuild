You've seen them at the hardware store: small plug-in devices that claim to drive out roaches, rodents and spiders using ultrasonic sound, with no chemicals and no traps. The pitch is appealing — if it sounds too good to be true, it's worth a closer look before spending the money.

## What the research actually shows

Ultrasonic pest repellers have been studied repeatedly, including by university entomology departments, and the results are consistent: most pests either aren't affected by the frequencies these devices emit, or they adapt to the sound within days and go right back to normal activity. Rodents in particular have been shown to habituate quickly. The sound also doesn't travel well around corners or through walls and furniture, so coverage in a real house is far more limited than the packaging suggests.

## Why they keep getting sold anyway

These devices are inexpensive to manufacture, easy to ship, and the claims are hard for a homeowner to disprove in the short term — if pest activity happens to be low for a few weeks, it's easy to credit the gadget. That's different from evidence that it actually caused the change.

## What we'd recommend instead

If a property has active pest pressure, the more useful first step is figuring out what's actually going on: where the activity is, what's attracting it, and what accessible entry points or conditions are contributing.
